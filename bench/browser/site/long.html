Harbor District Water Quality Review: Annual Report

Prepared by the Harbor District Utilities Board for residents, councillors and the regional regulator.

Summary

Over the reporting year the Harbor District delivered just over 41 million cubic metres of drinking water to roughly 212,000 people across four service zones. Every one of the 9,316 compliance samples collected at treatment outlets and customer taps met the microbiological standard, and 99.7 percent met every chemical and physical standard on the first test. The handful of exceptions, described in the exceptions section, were traced to a single reservoir inlet and a short stretch of unlined cast-iron main, both of which have since been repaired.

Demand fell by 2.4 percent compared with the previous year, which the board attributes to the smart metering rollout, a wetter summer and the continued replacement of leaking service connections. Non-revenue water, the share of treated water that never reaches a paying customer, fell from 14.1 to 12.8 percent. That figure is still above the board's long-term target of ten percent, and the network renewal programme remains the largest single item in the capital plan.

This report follows the structure agreed with the regulator two years ago: where the water comes from, how it is treated, how it travels to homes, how it is tested, what went wrong, and what the board intends to do next. Figures are for the twelve months ending in March unless stated otherwise.

Where the water comes from

Most of the district's supply, about 68 percent, is drawn from the Ashgrove reservoir, an upland impoundment fed by three moorland streams. The remainder comes from a group of eleven boreholes in the Millbrook sandstone aquifer and, during dry spells, a licensed river abstraction at Cotter's Weir. Each source has a distinct character. Reservoir water is soft and slightly acidic, carries natural organic colour after heavy rain and changes temperature with the seasons. Borehole water is hard, clear and stable in temperature, but some wells carry elevated iron and manganese. River water is the most variable of the three and is used only when reservoir storage drops below the trigger curve set in the drought plan.

Reservoir storage began the year at 87 percent and never fell below 61 percent, so the river abstraction was used on only nine days, all of them during planned maintenance at the main treatment works rather than for drought reasons. Catchment management continued in partnership with landowners on the moor. Peat restoration now covers 340 hectares, and the board's monitoring shows a measurable reduction in the colour load reaching the reservoir after storms on the restored sub-catchments compared with the unrestored ones. The catchment partnership has agreed to extend restoration to a further 120 hectares over the next three years.

Two of the eleven boreholes were rested for most of the year after routine sampling showed slowly rising nitrate concentrations. Neither breached the standard, but both are now blended with lower-nitrate wells before treatment, and the board has asked the regulator to review fertiliser practice on the farmland that overlies the aquifer recharge zone. Nitrate trends in the remaining wells were flat.

The Millbrook aquifer

The Millbrook sandstone is a confined aquifer for most of its extent, protected by a layer of glacial clay up to twenty metres thick. Where the clay thins, near the river terraces in the east of the district, the aquifer is more exposed to surface activity, and that is where the two rested boreholes sit. The board's hydrogeologist estimates that water now pumped from the deeper wells fell as rain between thirty and sixty years ago, which explains why changes in land use show up slowly and persist for a long time once they appear.

How the water is treated

Reservoir water is treated at the Ashgrove works, which can produce up to 140 megalitres a day. The process begins with coagulation, in which a ferric salt is dosed to gather fine particles and natural colour into larger flocs. The flocs are removed by dissolved air flotation, then the water passes through rapid gravity sand filters, a granular activated carbon stage for taste, odour and trace organics, and finally disinfection with chlorine. The pH is adjusted and a small dose of orthophosphate is added to reduce the dissolution of lead from older household pipework.

Borehole water is treated at three smaller sites. Each uses aeration and pressure filtration to remove iron and manganese, followed by ultraviolet disinfection and a low chlorine dose to protect the water in the network. The Hollins Lane site was refurbished this year: its filter media were replaced, the ultraviolet reactors were upgraded to a validated design, and a new telemetry outstation now reports turbidity and chlorine every thirty seconds instead of every fifteen minutes.

Total chemical use fell by six percent, mostly because lower colour in the raw water allowed coagulant doses to be trimmed for much of the autumn. Energy use at the treatment works rose slightly, by 1.8 percent, because the new ultraviolet reactors draw more power than the units they replaced. The board buys renewable electricity for all treatment sites and is testing a small hydro turbine on the reservoir compensation flow, discussed in the outlook.

Dissolved air flotation

Flotation suits upland water well because the flocs formed from natural colour are light and settle poorly. Air is dissolved under pressure into a recycle stream; when the pressure is released, millions of tiny bubbles attach to the flocs and carry them to the surface, where a scraper removes the resulting sludge blanket. The Ashgrove works has six flotation cells. Two were taken out of service in rotation for inspection this year, and both showed nozzle wear within the expected range.

How the water reaches homes

The distribution network comprises 1,870 kilometres of mains, 26 service reservoirs and water towers, and 41 pumping stations. About a fifth of the mains by length are unlined cast iron laid before 1960. These older pipes account for a disproportionate share of bursts, discoloured water complaints and leakage. The renewal programme replaced or relined 23 kilometres of main this year, slightly ahead of plan, prioritised using a model that combines burst history, soil corrosivity, pipe age and the number of customers that would lose supply in a failure.

The board recorded 311 bursts on mains, down from 356 the year before, and 1,940 repairs to leaking service pipes. Average time to repair a visible leak fell from 3.1 to 2.6 days. Customer contacts about discoloured water fell by eighteen percent, helped by a programme of controlled flushing in zones with a history of sediment build-up. Customers in the north zone, where most of the flushing took place, now report discolouration at roughly half the rate they did three years ago. The exceptions section describes one stretch of main where flushing alone was not enough.

Pressure management continued to expand. Fourteen new pressure-reducing valves were installed, mostly in hillside areas where night-time pressures had been well above what customers need. Lower pressure reduces both the frequency of bursts and the volume lost from leaks that have not yet been found. The board estimates that pressure management alone saved about 1.3 megalitres a day this year.

Smart metering

By year end 58 percent of households had a smart meter, up from 31 percent. The meters report hourly consumption and flag continuous flow that suggests a leak on the customer's side of the boundary. Over the year the board wrote to 4,212 households about a probable leak; follow-up readings show that about two thirds of those leaks were fixed within a month. Customers can view their own hourly use through the online account, and a pilot in the west zone is testing whether a short weekly summary changes behaviour more than the monthly bill does.

Telemetry

Every treatment site, service reservoir and pumping station now reports to a central control room. Alarms are triaged by duty controllers around the clock. The upgrade to faster reporting at the treatment sites means that a change in filtered water turbidity is seen within a minute rather than a quarter of an hour, which matters because the operating rules require a filter to be taken out of service quickly if its output deteriorates.

How the water is tested

The board's laboratory analysed 9,316 regulatory samples and a further 21,500 operational samples during the year. Regulatory samples are taken at random addresses within each zone according to a schedule agreed with the regulator, as well as at treatment works outlets and service reservoirs. Operational samples are taken wherever the operators need information, for example after a repair, when commissioning a new main, or when a customer reports a problem.

The laboratory holds accreditation for all regulatory parameters and took part in four external proficiency schemes, returning satisfactory results in every round. Two new instruments were commissioned: an ion chromatograph that measures nitrate, nitrite and bromate in one run, and an automated colony counter that shortens the time needed to read microbiological plates. Average time from sample collection to reported result fell from 2.4 to 1.9 days.

Results are published monthly on the board's website by zone, and any resident can ask for the most recent results for their own postcode. The appendix lists every parameter tested, the standard it is compared against and the number of samples taken.

Exceptions and incidents

Twenty-eight samples failed a chemical or physical standard on the first test. Nineteen of these were for iron and turbidity and came from customer taps on a 1.4 kilometre stretch of unlined cast-iron main in the north zone, where the pipe wall had corroded enough that flushing gave only temporary relief. The main was relined in November and follow-up samples have all passed. Six failures were for manganese at the outlet of the Kestrel Road service reservoir; investigation found a fault in an inlet valve that disturbed sediment when the reservoir filled quickly. The valve was replaced and the reservoir cleaned. The remaining three failures were isolated and could not be reproduced on resampling.

No sample failed the microbiological standard. The board reported two events to the regulator: a power failure at a pumping station that left 640 properties with low pressure for five hours, and the Kestrel Road manganese exceedances. The regulator closed both reports without further action, noting the speed of the response in each case.

Customer contacts about taste and odour rose briefly in late summer, when a bloom of algae in the reservoir produced compounds that some people can taste at extremely low concentrations. The compounds are harmless. The activated carbon stage removes most of them, and the dose of powdered carbon was raised for three weeks until the bloom subsided.

Lead pipes

The board does not own the pipes inside homes or the short private section between the property boundary and the house, but it has a strong interest in what happens there because lead from old service pipes is the most likely source of lead at the tap. An estimated 7,400 properties in the district still have a lead service pipe, most of them terraced houses built before 1920 in the central and north zones. Orthophosphate dosing, described under treatment, forms a protective layer on the inside of lead pipes and keeps lead concentrations well below the standard in almost all homes.

This year the board replaced the public section of 610 lead service pipes and offered a free replacement of the private section to every owner whose public pipe was being renewed. Uptake of the offer was 74 percent, higher than in any previous year, largely because the replacement was scheduled on the same day as the board's own work so that driveways and footpaths were dug only once. Schools and nurseries were prioritised: every school in the district has now been surveyed, and the last eleven lead connections serving schools were removed in the summer holidays.

Residents who think they may have a lead pipe can ask for a free tap sample. The board took 1,230 such samples this year. Four exceeded the standard, and in each case the household was supplied with a filter jug and offered priority replacement of both sections of the pipe.

Environmental performance

Treatment produces sludge, most of it iron and organic matter removed from reservoir water. All 6,900 tonnes produced this year were recycled to agricultural land under permit, after testing for metals and pathogens. The board also returned 2.1 million cubic metres of filter backwash water to the head of the Ashgrove works after settlement, reducing the volume drawn from the reservoir. Operational carbon emissions, counted on a location basis, fell by four percent, with most of the reduction coming from lower pumping energy as leakage and pressure fell.

Compensation flows from Ashgrove reservoir, the water released to keep the downstream river healthy, were maintained at or above the licensed minimum on every day of the year. A fish pass at Cotter's Weir, built jointly with the river trust, opened in September; counts in its first autumn recorded salmon and sea trout moving upstream of the weir for the first time in more than a century. The board will publish two years of monitoring data before deciding whether to fund a second pass further upstream.

Customers and affordability

Average household bills rose by 3.9 percent, below the rate of general inflation. The social tariff, which caps bills for households on low incomes, now supports 9,800 customers, an increase of 1,100 over the year. The board also extended its payment break scheme, which lets customers who have lost income pause payments for up to six months without penalty. Overall customer satisfaction, measured by an independent quarterly survey, was 87 percent, unchanged from the previous year.

Complaints handled through the formal process fell by eleven percent. The most common reasons for complaint were billing disputes after a meter exchange, the time taken to reinstate footpaths after repairs, and noise from night-time leak detection work. The board has changed its reinstatement contract so that temporary surfaces must be replaced within ten working days, down from twenty.

Outlook

The capital plan for the coming year totals 46 million, of which just over half goes to network renewal and pressure management. The rest covers a second refurbished borehole treatment site, the completion of the smart metering rollout, reservoir catchment restoration and a programme of upgrades to the control systems at the Ashgrove works. The board expects to reach ten percent non-revenue water within four years if the renewal rate is maintained.

The hydro turbine trial on the compensation flow from Ashgrove reservoir will run for twelve months. If it performs as modelled it will generate about a fifth of the electricity used by the treatment works, and the board will consider similar installations at two service reservoirs where water arrives under excess pressure. Results will be reported in next year's review.

Finally, the board will consult residents this spring on its long-term water resources plan, which looks fifty years ahead and considers population growth, climate change and the need to leave more water in the moorland streams for wildlife. The consultation documents will be available online and in every district library, and the board will hold public meetings in each of the four service zones.
